Origami robot · bench test 01

Crease Lab

Before a robot can fold origami, it has to understand paper. This bench runs a simplified sheet — it bends, slips on the desk, and remembers a crease — under three scripted hand moves.

Bench parameters

Mesh
1.0×
0.020

Finger speed and clearance shape scenario 3's pinch. A finer mesh folds more cleanly but costs more compute — the physics substep rate scales with resolution. Changing a parameter restarts the run.

Inside the model

Stretch
A square weave of point masses (625 at the default 25×25 mesh). Distance constraints keep the sheet inextensible, so it drapes and buckles instead of stretching.
Bend
Every mesh edge is a hinge that fights folding — a spring on the angle between neighbouring faces, solved with XPBD. The substep rate scales with mesh resolution so stiffness stays the same at every grid size.
Crease
Squeezed past its yield curvature under the fingertip, a hinge's preferred angle shifts permanently and stays shifted — plasticity. Scars draw as dark lines on the sheet.
Desk
A plane with static and kinetic friction — the sheet sticks until pushed hard enough, then slides (the whole story of scenario 2). The sheet's own layers rub on each other too, which is what keeps a fold from skating sideways while it is pressed.
Hands
Actuators are kinematic: a corner pin, point grips, and a capsule fingertip. All of their hardware — pads, rods, and the fingertip — collides with the sheet, so the paper can never pass through a tool. It answers with nothing but physics.

Deliberately simplified: creases can only form along mesh lines, creasing needs fingertip pressure (at this resolution a closed fold and a pressed crease read the same angle), self-contact is a simple particle cushion, and the air does nothing.
